## When to use it

Use this calculator to see how well an investment or project paid off. It works for shares, funds, property, a side business, a marketing campaign or new equipment. It turns two simple numbers, what you put in and what you got back, into a percentage you can compare with other choices.

## How to use it

1. Choose your **Currency**.
2. Enter the **Amount invested**, the total you put in.
3. Enter the **Amount returned (final value)**. Include any dividends, rent or other income you received along the way.
4. Optionally, enter the **Investment period** in years to see the annualized return.
5. Read the **Return on investment (ROI)**, the **Profit or loss**, the **Annualized return** and the **Value multiple**.

The value multiple tells you how many times your money grew. 1.5× means you got back one and a half times what you put in.

## Worked example

You invested $10,000 and three years later it is worth $13,500, including dividends. The ROI is +35%, a gain of $3,500. The value multiple is 1.35×.

With a period of 3 years, the annualized return is 10.52% a year. That is lower than 35 ÷ 3, about 11.7%, because each year's growth builds on the last.

Compare that with a savings account paying 4% a year: over three years it would turn $10,000 into about $11,249, so the investment did better, though with more risk.

## Tips

- **Always add the time period.** A total ROI on its own hides how long the money was tied up.
- **Count every cost.** Fees, repairs, software, shipping and your own time all reduce the real return.
- **Compare like with like.** Check the annualized return of each option over the same period.
- **Use it before you invest.** Enter a hopeful and a cautious estimate of the amount returned to see the range of outcomes.

## Reading a loss

If the amount returned is lower than the amount invested, the ROI shows as a negative number and the value multiple drops below 1×. For example, $10,000 that is now worth $8,000 is a 20% loss and a multiple of 0.8×.

## Common mistakes

- **Dividing ROI by the years.** Simple division overstates the yearly rate. Use the annualized figure instead.
- **Leaving out income received.** Forgetting dividends or rent makes the return look smaller than it was.
- **Ignoring risk.** A higher ROI is not always better if it came with a big chance of losing money.

Results are estimates for general information only. Past returns do not guarantee future results.
