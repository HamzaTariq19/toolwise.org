## When to use it

Use this calculator to turn a salary into an hourly rate, or an hourly rate into a yearly salary. It helps when comparing job offers, setting freelance rates, checking that you are paid at least the minimum wage, or seeing what extra hours are really worth.

## How to use it

1. Choose your **Currency**.
2. Enter your **Pay**, then choose how often you get it next to **per**: year, month, 2 weeks, week, day or hour.
3. Enter your **Hours per week** and **Days per week**.
4. Enter your **Paid weeks per year**. Use 52 if your holidays are paid.
5. Read your **Hourly pay**, plus the daily, weekly, every 2 weeks, monthly and yearly figures.

All results are before tax and deductions.

## Worked example

You are offered a salary of $52,000 a year for a 40-hour, 5-day week with paid holidays. The calculator shows $25.00 an hour, $200 a day, $1,000 a week, $2,000 every two weeks and $4,333.33 a month.

Now compare it with a contract job paying $30 an hour. Choose **per hour** and enter 30. At 40 hours a week over 52 weeks, that is $62,400 a year. But if the contract has no paid holidays and you plan to take four weeks off, set **Paid weeks per year** to 48. The yearly figure drops to $57,600, still more than the salary, but you would need to cover your own benefits.

## Part-time work

For part-time jobs, enter the hours and days you actually work. A $24,000 salary for 20 hours a week over 52 paid weeks works out at about $23.08 an hour.

## Tips

- **Use your real hours.** If your contract says 37.5 hours, enter 37.5. If you regularly stay late unpaid, try your actual hours to see your true rate.
- **For freelance rates, aim higher.** Freelancers pay for their own equipment, time off and quiet periods, so a matching hourly rate is usually much higher than an employee's.
- **Check the minimum wage.** Compare your hourly figure with the legal minimum where you work.

## Common mistakes

- **Multiplying a weekly wage by 4 for a month.** A month averages about 4.33 weeks. The calculator uses the correct figure.
- **Comparing gross with net pay.** Make sure both numbers are before tax, or both are after.
- **Ignoring benefits.** Health cover, pension contributions and paid leave are worth money. A higher hourly rate without them may not be better.
