## When to use it

Use this tool to pick random numbers for games, raffles, giveaways, classroom activities, sampling, statistics exercises or just settling a choice. It can pick one number or many, with or without repeats, as whole numbers or decimals.

## How to use it

1. Enter the range in **From** and **To**.
2. Set **How many numbers** you want, from 1 to 1,000.
3. Choose **Decimal places**: **None (whole numbers)**, 1, 2 or 3.
4. Tick **No repeats (every number different)** and **Sort from smallest to largest** if needed.
5. Or tap a preset: **1–10**, **1–100**, **Dice 1–6** or **Lottery 6 of 49**.
6. Press **Generate**, then **Copy** to copy the result.

## Worked examples

**Picking a raffle winner:** you sold tickets numbered 1 to 250. Set From 1, To 250, how many 1, and press **Generate**. The number shown is the winning ticket. For three prizes, set how many to 3 and tick **No repeats**, so the same ticket cannot win twice.

**Lottery numbers:** tap **Lottery 6 of 49**. The tool picks six different numbers from 1 to 49 and sorts them, for example 9, 11, 24, 26, 47 and 48.

**Classroom:** with 28 students numbered on the register, generate one number from 1 to 28 to choose who answers next.

## Why not just pick a number yourself?

People are poor at being random. When asked for a number from 1 to 10, many choose 7, and we tend to avoid repeats and patterns that real randomness produces all the time. A generator removes that bias, which matters for fair draws, surveys and science experiments.

## Tips

- **Write down the settings first.** For a public draw, announce the range and rules before you press Generate.
- **Use No repeats for groups.** To split 30 people into teams, generate all 30 numbers without repeats and take them in order.
- **Copy the result straight away.** Each press of Generate gives new numbers.
- **Sort long lists.** Sorted numbers are easier to read and check.

## Common mistakes

- **Asking for more unique numbers than the range holds.** You cannot pick 10 different numbers from 1 to 5.
- **Thinking some numbers are "due".** Each draw is independent. A number that has not appeared for a while is no more likely next time.
- **Forgetting decimals affect the range.** From 1 to 2 with one decimal place gives values like 1.3 or 1.9, not just 1 or 2.
