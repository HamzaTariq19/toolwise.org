## When to use it

The compound interest calculator shows how savings or investments grow over time when interest is added back to the balance. Use it to see what regular saving could become by retirement, to compare savings accounts, to decide between saving more or starting earlier, or to explain to a teenager why starting early matters so much.

## How to calculate compound interest

1. Choose your **Currency**.
2. Enter the **Starting amount** you have now.
3. Enter a **Monthly contribution**, the amount you'll add each month (0 if none).
4. Enter the **Annual interest rate** and the number of **Years**.
5. Choose **Compounding**: **Yearly**, **Quarterly**, **Monthly** or **Daily**.
6. Read the **Future value**, **Total deposits** and **Interest earned**. The bar shows how much came from deposits and how much from interest.
7. Press **Show** under **Year-by-year breakdown** for each year's figures.

## A worked example

You have $10,000 saved and can add $200 a month. At 7% a year, compounded monthly, for 20 years, the result is **$144,572.72**. Only **$58,000** of that is money you put in; **$86,573** is interest. In the breakdown you'll see the interest earned each year grow from about $800 in year one to over $9,600 in the final year, which is compounding at work.

## What makes the biggest difference

- **Time:** wait 10 years longer and the same habit often more than doubles the result, because growth builds on growth.
- **Rate:** the gap between 4% and 7% looks small but can mean tens of thousands over 20 to 30 years.
- **Regular contributions:** adding a little every month often matters more than a big starting amount.
- **Fees:** a 1% yearly fee comes straight off the rate, so 7% becomes 6%.

## Tips

- **Start with realistic rates.** Savings accounts and bonds pay steadier but lower rates; shares vary year to year.
- **Test several scenarios,** such as a cautious, a middle and an optimistic rate.
- **Increase contributions when your income rises,** even by small amounts.
- **Think in today's money** by lowering the rate by your expected inflation.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating results as guaranteed.** Investment returns can fall as well as rise.
- **Entering the monthly rate** instead of the annual rate. Always use the yearly figure.
- **Forgetting tax and fees,** which reduce real returns.
- **Comparing accounts by rate alone** when compounding and fees differ; compare the future value instead.
